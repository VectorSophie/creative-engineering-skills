# Cite What You Read

A citation is evidence only when the source exists, the metadata is right, and the cited source actually supports the sentence attached to it. A plausible title in a bibliography is not scholarship; it is autocomplete with consequences.

## 1. Verify the Source Before You Cite It

**Existence comes before usefulness.**

- Confirm the paper or source through a primary or authoritative index, and prefer the published version over a preprint when they materially differ.
- Verify authors, title, venue, year, and a stable identifier such as DOI or arXiv ID before treating an entry as final.
- Never manufacture BibTeX or bibliographic metadata from memory. If verification is unavailable, leave an explicit placeholder instead of guessing.

Failure signal: the citation looks convincing but you cannot point to where its metadata came from.

## 2. Verify the Claim, Not Just the Paper

**A real paper can still be cited falsely.**

- Read enough of the source to confirm the specific proposition you are using it to support.
- Distinguish what the authors demonstrated from what they hypothesized, discussed, or cited from somebody else.
- For quantitative comparisons, verify the metric, dataset, setting, and evaluation protocol rather than copying an isolated number.

The test: if a reviewer opened the cited source at the relevant passage, would they find the same claim you just made?

## 3. Keep Citation States Distinct

**Found, read, and verified are different states.**

- Track references as at least discovered, read, or claim-verified. Do not let a search result silently graduate into evidence.
- Keep short evidence notes for important sources: headline claim, method, relevant result, limitation, and why this paper cites it.
- Re-open the source when the wording of your claim changes; an old verification does not automatically support a stronger sentence.

## 4. Cite the Closest Evidence

**Prefer the source that actually establishes the point.**

- Cite original work for a method or result when practical, not a later paper that merely mentions it.
- Use surveys for synthesis and field-level context, not as a substitute for primary evidence behind a precise technical claim.
- When several works support different parts of a sentence, split the sentence or place citations where each claim is actually supported.

## 5. Make Uncertainty Visible

**An honest missing citation is safer than a fabricated finished one.**

- Mark unresolved references and unverified claims explicitly so they cannot be mistaken for completed scholarship.
- Record corrections when you discover wrong metadata or a misattributed claim instead of silently preserving the error downstream.
- Never strengthen prose to hide weak literature support; weaken the claim or keep searching.

## The final test

You can defend every important citation by answering two questions independently: how do you know this source is real, and where does it support this exact claim?
